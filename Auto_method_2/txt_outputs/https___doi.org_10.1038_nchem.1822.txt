# Exceptionally fast carbon–carbon bond reductive elimination from gold(III)

  * William J. Wolf1 na1, 
  * Matthew S. Winston1 na1 &
  * F. Dean Toste1 

[_Nature Chemistry_](/nchem) **volume  6**, pages 159–164 (2014)Cite this
article

  * 12k Accesses

  * 11 Altmetric

## Abstract

Reductive elimination of carbon–carbon bonds occurs in numerous metal-
catalysed reactions. This process is well documented for a variety of
transition metal complexes. However, carbon–carbon bond reductive elimination
from a limited number of Au(III) complexes has been shown to be a slow and
prohibitive process that generally requires elevated temperatures. Herein we
show that oxidation of a series of mono- and bimetallic Au(I) aryl complexes
at low temperature generates observable Au(III) and Au(II) intermediates. We
also show that aryl–aryl bond reductive elimination from these oxidized
species is not only among the fastest observed for any transition metal, but
is also mechanistically distinct from previously studied alkyl–alkyl and
aryl–alkyl reductive eliminations from Au(III).

biaryl reductive elimination from non-A-frame bimetallic gold complexes.

## Results and discussion

### Biaryl reductive elimination from a mononuclear gold complex

 _cis_ -Ph3PAu(4-F-C6H4)2Cl (**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)**) was
synthesized on partial oxidation of Ph3PAu(4-F-C6H4)
(**[1](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/1)**) with PhICl2 at −78 °C, followed by
fast transmetallation from the remaining
**[1](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/1)** to Ph3PAu(4-F-C6H4)Cl2
(**[2](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/2)**) ([Fig.
1](/articles/nchem.1822#Fig1)). The reaction is unaffected by equimolar or
excess amounts of oxidant, which indicates that transmetallation from
**[1](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/1)** to
**[2](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/2)** is faster than oxidation of
**[1](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/1)**. Although
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** cannot be isolated, the _cis_ -aryl
relationship is supported by 19F NMR (two 1:1 singlets at −118.4, −119.5 ppm)
and 31P NMR (one singlet at 27.9 ppm) spectroscopy.

**Figure 1: Oxidation of monometallic Au(I) and biaryl reductive elimination
from Au(III).**

[](/articles/nchem.1822/figures/1)

A CD2Cl2 solution of Ph3PAu(4-F-C6H4)
(**[1](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/1)**) undergoes fast partial oxidation
by PhICl2 on thawing at low temperature to afford
**[2](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/2)** , which reacts rapidly with the
remaining **[1](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/1)** to generate a _cis_
-diaryl Au(III) species (**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)**). Reductive
elimination from **[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** affords
4,4′-difluorobiphenyl. Associative ligand exchange of
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** with excess PPh3 generates cationic
**[4](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/4)** , which undergoes very fast biaryl
reductive elimination.

[Full size image](/articles/nchem.1822/figures/1)

In light of Kochi's findings, we expected biaryl reductive elimination from
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** to require heating via a mechanism
that involved phosphine dissociation. However, to our surprise,
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** was immediately consumed at room
temperature (20 °C), and quantitatively generated 4,4′-difluorobiphenyl and
(Ph3P)AuCl. At −52 °C, aryl–aryl reductive elimination from
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** could be monitored by 19F NMR (_k_
obs = (1.5 ± 0.1) × 10−4 s−1) ([Fig. 2](/articles/nchem.1822#Fig2)); at −23
°C, reductive elimination is 100 times faster (_k_ obs = 0.015 ± 0.001 s−1).
The observed rate constant remains unchanged over a range of concentrations,
as well as in the presence of 10 equiv. of either Bu4NCl or Ph3PAuCl, which
indicates a unimolecular process that is first order in
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)**.

That reductive elimination is substantially more facile from
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** than from alkylgold(III) complexes
may not be attributed simply to faster coupling of aryl–aryl relative to
alkyl–alkyl bonds[9](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR9 "Low, J. J. & Goddard, W. A.
Theoretical studies of oxidative addition and reductive elimination. 3.
Carbon–hydrogen and carbon–carbon reductive coupling from palladium and
platinum bis\(phosphine\) complexes. J. Am. Chem. Soc. 108, 6115–6128
\(1986\)."), but suggests fundamentally different rate-determining steps or
mechanisms. Contrary to that established for alkyl reductive elimination of
Au(III), excess PPh3 increases the observed rate of reductive elimination
([Fig. 3](/articles/nchem.1822#Fig3)). In the presence of 10 equiv. of PPh3 at
−52 °C, the rate of reductive elimination from
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** increases by an order of magnitude
(_k_ obs = (1.2 ± 0.2) × 10−3 s−1); with 40 equiv., the observed pseudo-first
order rate constant is (6.2 ± 0.5) × 10−3 s−1. No new 19F and 31P NMR signals
were observed under these conditions. In contrast, neither Bu4NCl nor radical
traps, such as 9,10-dihydroanthracene, affect reductive elimination of
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** ; furthermore, the rate remains
unchanged in the presence of excess oxidant (PhICl2 acts as a phosphine
scavenger), which precludes the scenario in which small amounts of PPh3
liberated from **[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** induce reductive
elimination. These observations are consistent with two operative pathways to
biaryl: one independent of added PPh3 and another that involves phosphine
coordination. In the absence of added phosphine, reductive elimination occurs
directly from the square-planar complex
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)**. In the presence of excess PPh3,
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** probably undergoes associative
ligand exchange to generate cation **[4](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/4)** ,
which rapidly reductively eliminates biaryl. This process is first order in
PPh3 ([Fig. 3b](/articles/nchem.1822#Fig3)). As
**[4](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/4)** is unobservable by NMR spectroscopy,
reductive elimination from this complex must be substantially faster than that
from **[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** , given its bulkier steric
environment and cationic charge. When 10 equiv. of PPh3 and 40 equiv. of
Bu4NCl are added, the rate remains unchanged with respect to that of the
phosphine-accelerated reaction, which suggests that reductive elimination from
**[4](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/4)** is substantially faster than the
reverse ligand exchange. At −52 °C, association of PPh3 (_k_  = 0.019 ± 0.001
s−1 M−1) and reductive elimination from
**[4](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/4)** (_k_  ≥ 0.22 s−1, see [Supplementary
Information](/articles/nchem.1822#MOESM233)) are remarkably facile processes.
We cannot definitively discount the possibility of reductive elimination from
a five-coordinate intermediate after coordination of PPh3, but this scenario
is improbable. Presumably, alkyl Au(III) complexes should behave similarly in
the presence of excess PPh3, but their reductive eliminations are, instead,
drastically slowed. Also, although Yamamoto has demonstrated that a _cis-_
arylmethylnickel(II) complex with a chelating bis(phosphine) undergoes
accelerated reductive elimination in the presence of PPh3 via a five-
coordinate species[3](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR3 "Komiya, S., Abe, Y.,
Yamamoto, A. & Yamamoto, T. Phosphine-induced reductive elimination from cis-
arylmethylnickel\(II\) complexes having a 1,2-bis\(dimethylphosphino\)ethane
ligand. Organometallics 2, 1466–1468 \(1983\)."), comparisons with complex
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** are imperfect because of the
potentially labile Au–Cl bond.

To our knowledge, reductive eliminations from diarylgold(III) complexes
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** and
**[4](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/4)** are among the fastest observed C–C
bond-forming reductive couplings at any transition metal centre at
temperatures below −20 °C. For comparison, reported rate constants for
reductive elimination of diarylplatinum(II)[19](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR19
"Shekhar, S. & Hartwig, J. F. Distinct electronic effects on reductive
eliminations of symmetrical and unsymmetrical bis-aryl platinum complexes. J.
Am. Chem. Soc. 126, 13016–13027 \(2004\).") and
diarylpalladium(II)[12](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR12 "Osakada, K., Onodera,
H. & Nishihara, Y. Diarylpalladium complexes with a cis structure. Formation
via transmetalation of arylboronic acids with an aryliodopalladium complex and
intramolecular coupling of the aryl ligands, affording unsymmetrical biaryls.
Organometallics 24, 190–192 \(2005\).") complexes are between 10−5 to 10−3 s−1
at 95 and 50 °C, respectively, although palladium-catalysed Kumada–Corriu
couplings can be achieved at temperatures as low as −65 °C (ref.
[43](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR43 "Martin, R. & Buchwald, S. L. Pd-catalyzed
Kumada–Corriu cross-coupling reactions at low temperatures allow the use of
Knochel-type Grignard reagents J. Am. Chem. Soc. 129, 3844–3845 \(2007\).")).
In some cases, diarylplatinum(II) systems only reductively eliminate biaryl at
temperatures above 100 °C (ref. [19](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR19 "Shekhar,
S. & Hartwig, J. F. Distinct electronic effects on reductive eliminations of
symmetrical and unsymmetrical bis-aryl platinum complexes. J. Am. Chem. Soc.
126, 13016–13027 \(2004\).")). Reductive elimination rate constants of ∼0.1
s−1 have also been reported for nickel-catalysed oxidative homocouplings at
−35 °C (ref. [6](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR6 "Jin, L., Zhang, H., Li, P.,
Sowa, J. R. Jr & Lei, A. What is the rate of the Csp2–Csp2 reductive
elimination step? Revealing an unusually fast Ni-catalyzed Negishi-type
oxidative coupling reaction. J. Am. Chem. Soc. 131, 9892–9893 \(2009\).")),
although the presumed diarylnickel(II) intermediate was not observed. Indeed,
for a third-row metal, the rates of aryl–aryl reductive elimination from
Au(III) are particularly impressive, outcompeting even C–C bond coupling in
alkynylarylpalladium(IV) complexes[44](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR44 "Canty,
A. J. Organopalladium and platinum chemistry in oxidising milieu as models for
organic synthesis involving the higher oxidation states of palladium. Dalton
Trans. 10409–10417 \(2009\).") (_k_  ≈ 10−3 s−1 at −35 °C). An Eyring analysis
over a 29 °C range reveals that the reductive elimination from
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** not only has an unusually small
enthalpic barrier, but a small entropic contribution to the activation energy
as well (Δ _H_ ‡ = 17.2 ± 0.2 kcal mol−1, Δ _S_ ‡ = 2.0 ± 0.8 e.u., see
[Supplementary Information](/articles/nchem.1822#MOESM233)); these kinetic
parameters suggest a transition state that resembles
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)** in which the Au–C(_sp_ 2) bonds are
mostly conserved.

It is not immediately obvious why aryl and chloride ligands drastically change
the mechanism of C–C bond reductive elimination at Au(III). Generally, the
barrier to aryl–aryl reductive elimination is lower than that for alkyl–alkyl
reductive elimination[8](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR8 "Moravskiy, A. & Stille,
J. K. Mechanisms of 1,1-reductive elimination from palladium: elimination of
ethane from dimethylpalladium\(II\) and trimethylpalladium\(IV\). J. Am. Chem.
Soc. 103, 4182–4186 \(1981\)."), and the weaker _σ_ -donating ability of aryl
and chloride ligands relative to that of alkyls may increase the barrier to
phosphine dissociation. The notion that ‘Au(III) and Pt(II) catalysts show
similar reactivity’[42](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR42 "Gorin, D. J. & Toste,
F. D. Relativistic effects in homogeneous gold catalysis. Nature 446, 395–403
\(2007\).") is not necessarily true when redox processes are involved: at
greater than 90 °C, Pt(II) biaryl complexes undergo reductive elimination at
rates comparable to those observed for Au(III) at approximately −50 °C. The
reason for this discrepancy in rates may lie in pronounced relativistic
effects, which result in Au(I) having a strong preference for linearity over
other transition metals[42](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR42 "Gorin, D. J. &
Toste, F. D. Relativistic effects in homogeneous gold catalysis. Nature 446,
395–403 \(2007\)."). As the product of reductive elimination from a square
planar complex is necessarily two-coordinate, and the transition state is
partway to linearity, the activation energy of reductive elimination of
Au(III) to Au(I) may be less than that for other transition metals. This is
reasonable given the early transition state to reductive elimination of
**[3](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/3)**.

To assess the role of metal–metal bonding in the C–C bond reductive
elimination of oxidized gold, we prepared a bimetallic Au(I) complex
stabilized by a bis(diphenylphosphinoamine) ligand, Ph2P–NR–PPh2
(PNP)[46](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR46 "Wass, D. F. et al. High activity
ethylene trimerisation catalysts based on diphosphine ligands. Chem. Commun.
858–859 \(2002\)."),[47](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR47 "Esswein, A. J., Veige,
A. S. & Nocera, D. G. A photocycle for hydrogen production from two-electron
mixed-valence complexes. J. Am. Chem. Soc. 127, 16641–16651 \(2005\)."). X-ray
diffraction analysis of PNP[Au(4-F-C6H4)]2
(**[5](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/5)** , R=CH3) indicates an aurophilic
interaction (Au(I)–Au(I) distance = 3.0357(2) Å) that is probably conserved in
solution (see [Supplementary Information](/articles/nchem.1822#MOESM233)).
Laguna has shown that direct oxidation of PNP[Au(C6F5)]2 (R=H) with Cl2
generates the symmetric bimetallic Au(II) complex PNP[Au(C6F5)Cl]2, which is
stable at room temperature; crystallographic analysis established a
Au(II)–Au(II) bond (2.576(2) Å) and a _trans_ relationship between chloride
ligands[38](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR38 "Usón, R., Laguna, A., Laguna, M. &
Fraile, M. N. Mono- and bi-nuclear gold\(I\), gold\(II\), and gold\(III\)
perhalogenoaryl complexes with the ligand bis\(diphenylphosphino\)amine.
Crystal and molecular structure of
μ-\[bis\(diphenylphosphino\)amine\]-dichlorobis\(pentafluorophenyl\)digold\(II\).
J. Chem. Soc. Dalton Trans. 291–296 \(1986\).").

The analogous low-temperature oxidation of
**[5](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/5)** with PhICl2 generates the symmetric
bimetallic Au(II) complex **[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)** (19F NMR
and 31P NMR singlets at −120.4 ppm and 83.5 ppm, respectively), which
undergoes slow reductive elimination at temperatures below −30 °C ([Fig.
4](/articles/nchem.1822#Fig4)). At −23 °C,
**[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)** undergoes first-order decay (_k_  =
(1.6 ± 0.3) × 10−4 s−1) with concomitant formation of 4,4′-difluorobiphenyl.
Without observing a mixed-valent intermediate, we cannot distinguish
kinetically between a pathway that involves rearrangement and reductive
elimination, and one that involves bimetallic reductive elimination via a
four-centred transition state[48](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR48 "Whitesides,
G. M., Casey, C. P. & Krieger, J. K. The thermal decomposition of vinylic
copper\(I\) and silver\(I\) organometallic compounds. J. Am. Chem. Soc. 93,
1379–1389 \(1971\)."). However, Laguna has reported that similar PNP-supported
binuclear perfluoroarylgold(II) complexes rearrange over several hours to
mixed-valent Au(I)/Au(III) species[38](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR38 "Usón,
R., Laguna, A., Laguna, M. & Fraile, M. N. Mono- and bi-nuclear gold\(I\),
gold\(II\), and gold\(III\) perhalogenoaryl complexes with the ligand
bis\(diphenylphosphino\)amine. Crystal and molecular structure of
μ-\[bis\(diphenylphosphino\)amine\]-dichlorobis\(pentafluorophenyl\)digold\(II\).
J. Chem. Soc. Dalton Trans. 291–296 \(1986\)."). Given the fast aryl
transmetallation and reductive elimination of mononuclear Au(III), as well as
the scarcity of reported binuclear reductive elimination, we favour a
mechanism that involves isomerization of
**[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)** to mixed-valent species
**[7](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/7)** , which then undergoes fast,
unobservable reductive elimination. The kinetic parameters for the rate-
limiting isomerization of **[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)** to
**[7](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/7)** (Δ _H_ ‡ = 16.9 ± 0.4 kcal mol−1; Δ
_S_ ‡ = −7.8 ± 1.6 e.u.) are reflective of a low-barrier process with a small
entropic penalty (see [Supplementary
Information](/articles/nchem.1822#MOESM233)). This step is first order in
**[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)** and unaffected by excess Bu4NCl.
Complex **[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)** also oxidizes excess ligand,
presumably via chloronium transfer; therefore, phosphine dissociation is
probably not a prerequisite for either isomerization or reductive elimination,
because PNP(AuCl)2 is formed quantitatively when no excess ligand is added,
and no decomposition of liberated ligand (PNP +
**[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)**) is observed. These results are
consistent with a mechanism that involves intramolecular aryl transfer from
**[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)** to generate
**[7](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/7)** , which then directly reductively
eliminates to PNP(AuCl)2 and 4,4′-difluorobiphenyl.

**Figure 4: Oxidation of bimetallic Au(I)/Au(I) complexes.**

[](/articles/nchem.1822/figures/4)

This is under kinetic control, and affords Au(II)/Au(II) intermediates that
isomerize to mixed-valent Au(I)/Au(III) species and reductively eliminate
biaryl from a single metal centre. Solutions of PNP[Au(4-F-C6H4)]2
(**[5](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/5)**) and dppm[Au(4-F-C6H4)]2
(**[8](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/8)**) undergo fast oxidation by PhICl2
on thawing to generate intermediates stabilized by intramolecular
Au(II)–Au(II) bonds. Complex **[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)**
undergoes isomerization more slowly than
**[9](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/9)** , perhaps because of decreased
ligand flexibility. As isomerization for PNP-supported complexes is rate-
limiting, reductive elimination from **[7](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/7)**
is not observable. In contrast, the rates of isomerization and reductive
elimination of dppm-supported complexes are comparable, which allows kinetic
analysis of both steps to preclude the possibility of a bimetallic reductive
elimination directly from **[9](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/9)**.

[Full size image](/articles/nchem.1822/figures/4)

We reasoned that N → P _π_ -donation in PNP-type ligands results in a barrier
to N–P bond rotation that becomes significant at low
temperature[49](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR49 "Goldwhite, H. & Rowsell, D. G.
Rotational barriers about phosphorus–nitrogen bonds. J. Chem. Soc. D. 713
\(1969\)."). A more flexible ligand, such as 1,1-bis(diphenylphosphino)methane
(dppm), could presumably facilitate intramolecular aryl transfer from a
bimetallic Au(II) intermediate, which results in a lower barrier to
isomerization. Oxidation of dppm[Au(4-F-C6H4)2]2
(**[8](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/8)**) at −78 °C by PhICl2 affords
symmetric bimetallic Au(II) species **[9](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/9)**
(19F NMR and 31P NMR singlets at −120.3 ppm and −18.2 ppm, respectively),
which is consumed faster than the analogous PNP complex
**[6](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/6)** ([Fig.
4](/articles/nchem.1822#Fig4)). Indeed, at −52 °C,
**[9](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/9)** undergoes first-order decay, with
the fast appearance and slow consumption of mixed-valent Au(I)/Au(III) complex
**[10](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/10)** , and the appearance of
4,4′-difluorobiphenyl at approximately the same rate as the disappearance of
**[9](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/9)** ([Fig.
5](/articles/nchem.1822#Fig5)). Kinetic modelling precludes any scenario in
which a bimetallic reductive elimination occurs directly from
**[9](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/9)**. Instead,
**[9](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/9)** probably undergoes irreversible
isomerization to **[10](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/10)** (_k_  = (4.6 ±
0.1) × 10−4 s−1), which then reductively eliminates 4,4′-difluorobiphenyl at a
comparable rate (_k_  = 3.9 × 10−4 s−1). At −30 °C, both reactions are
immeasurably fast. Although PNP-supported Au(I)/Au(III) species
**[7](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/7)** could not be observed, these
findings support a similar reductive elimination mechanism of PNP-supported
complexes that involves the intermediacy of such mixed-valent species, as
proposed above.

Using the dppm-supported system discussed above as a benchmark, we probed the
behaviour of a bimetallic system with a five-atom linker that could perturb
intramolecular metal–metal interactions. We envisioned that a longer linker
(such as 1,3-bis(diphenylphosphino)propane (dppp)) between gold atoms might
preclude formation of a seven-membered metallacyclic Au(II)–Au(II)
intermediate on oxidation. Indeed, the only product of low-temperature
oxidation of dppp[Au(4-F-C6H4)]2 (**[11](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/11)**)
by PhICl2 is mixed-valent species **[13](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/13)**
(two 1:1 19F NMR singlets at −118.3 and −118.6 ppm and two 1:1 31P NMR
singlets at 27.3 and 21.7 ppm), presumably formed via fast isomerization of
**[12](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/12)** ([Fig.
6](/articles/nchem.1822#Fig6)). Even at −80 °C, no signals consistent with a
symmetric bimetallic Au(II) species were observed, suggestive that a mixed-
valent species is formed directly on oxidation. On warming to −52 °C,
**[13](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/13)** undergoes first-order reductive
elimination to dppp(AuCl)2 and 4,4′-difluorobiphenyl (_k_  = (3.0 ± 0.1) ×
10−5 s−1). Hence, the kinetic profiles of oxidation and reductive elimination
of bimetallic complexes bearing linkers that restrict metal–metal interactions
resemble those of mononuclear species.

Importantly, these studies reveal that access to a kinetic bimetallic Au(II)
product of oxidation does not necessarily compromise the rate of reductive
elimination from Au(III); in fact, the rate of reductive elimination from
dppm-supported **[10](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/10)** is about an order
of magnitude faster than that of **[13](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/13)** ,
perhaps because of increased steric crowding as a result of a shorter linker,
aurophilic interactions between the gold centres in
**[10](/articles/nchem.1822/compounds/10)** or a combination thereof. Even
Au(II)/Au(II) → Au(I)/Au(III) isomerization for PNP- and dppm-supported
complexes is a facile process at temperatures below 0 °C.

## Conclusion

We uncovered several unexpected properties of oxidized gold that are contrary
to established ideas. First, C–C bond reductive elimination from Au(III) does
not necessarily require phosphine dissociation. By avoiding this process,
biaryl reductive eliminations from Au(III) are remarkably fast, and occur as
low as −52 °C. Furthermore, by judicious choice of ligand, the barrier to
oxidation of bimetallic Au(I) complexes may be reduced, which allows access to
symmetric intermediates with Au(II)–Au(II) bonds. When stabilized by dppm,
these complexes undergo fast isomerization and reductive elimination at very
low temperatures; when stabilized by more rigid PNP-type ligands, these
reactions may be arrested, although at slightly higher temperatures
isomerization and reductive elimination become facile.

The stoichiometric behaviour of the complexes reported in this study informs
the development of catalysis that involves Au(I)/Au(III) redox cycling. For
instance, that phosphine dissociation is not required for C–C bond formation
may slow or entirely preclude deleterious ligand oxidation by excess oxidant
under conditions relevant to catalysis. Efforts to develop gold-catalysed
transformations using relatively weak oxidants compatible with substrates
should focus on bimetallic Au(I) precatalysts with three-atom linkers, such as
PNP or dppm. Given that transmetallation to Au(I) can be achieved at
temperatures as low as −78 °C (ref. [50](/articles/nchem.1822#ref-CR50 "Usón,
R., Laguna, A. & Vicente, J. Novel anionic gold\(I\) and gold\(III\)
organocomplexes. J. Organomet. Chem. 131, 471–475 \(1977\).")), and that Au(I)
oxidation, isomerization and reductive elimination are facile processes below
0 °C, catalysis may be achieved at temperatures low enough to avoid unwanted
side reactions between oxidant and substrate. Investigations to probe ligand
and electronic effects on the reductive elimination at Au(III), as well as the
stoichiometric reactivity of bimetallic Au(II) complexes, are ongoing to
further our nascent understanding of the fundamental chemistry of oxidized
gold.
